I didn't plan to become a business analyst. I studied Computer Science and Informatics, started out writing code, and took a few turns before I landed in banking. Looking back, every role taught me something I now use every week. Here's what each one gave me.

## Developer: most bugs start before the code

My first job was as a graduate software developer at Infomage Rims Group, building features and fixing bugs in C#, SQL and Python for two learning platforms.

What stayed with me was how often a "bug" turned out to be a misunderstanding. The feature worked exactly as written; it just wasn't what the user needed. I also helped gather requirements for new features, and I started to notice that the clearer the requirement, the fewer surprises in testing.

That's still how I think about requirements today. A requirement nobody can test isn't finished.

## Data capturer: fix the process, not just the error

At Shell Energy I moved paper records onto database systems. It was simple work, and it taught me a lesson I come back to all the time.

Correcting mistakes one by one kept me busy. Looking at where the mistakes came from, and changing how data was captured, actually reduced them. Data quality isn't something you add at the end. It starts with how the work is done.

## Operations: the numbers tell you where, people tell you why

At Bolt I worked in operations, going through supply numbers and ride trends to find where performance was slipping, and building dashboards for the team.

The dashboards showed us *where* things were going wrong. They didn't explain *why*. When driver engagement dropped during a tough period in the market, the answer wasn't in a chart. It came from understanding how drivers were experiencing the platform. I learned to treat the numbers as the starting point, and then go and talk to the people involved.

## Junior business analyst: write it so IT can build it and the business can sign it off

At Sasfin Bank I became a business analyst properly. I ran requirements and JAD workshops, wrote business requirements, user stories and acceptance criteria, documented processes and SOPs, and wrote the test cases the business used to sign off.

This is where I learned that a BA works for two audiences at once. The requirement has to be clear enough for IT to build from, and close enough to the business need that the people who asked for it recognise it. I also learned to bring Risk and Compliance in early. In a bank, a process that breaks a control will be sent back, however well it works.

## Business analyst: people, process and technology

Today I'm a business analyst focused on process and operational analysis. Most of my work is understanding how things actually get done: who does each step, how the work flows, and which systems support it.

That's become my lens for every problem: **people, process and technology**. If you change the system but not the process, people find workarounds. If you redesign the process but don't agree who owns each step, it drifts back. The change only sticks when all three move together.

## What ties it together

Looking back, each role taught me one side of that lens. Development taught me technology and how it gets built. Data capture and operations taught me process and what happens when it breaks. Business analysis taught me people: getting everyone to agree on the need before anyone talks about solutions.

If you're early in your career and wondering whether a detour is wasted time, my experience says it isn't. Every role I've had made me a better analyst. I just didn't know it at the time.
