When I finished my BSc in Computer Science and Informatics at the University of Johannesburg in 2018, I was proud and a little nervous, but mostly sure of one thing: I was going to write software. That's what the degree was for, and that's what I'd spent three years preparing to do.

It didn't work out that way. For a while that scared me. Today I'm grateful for it.

## Varsity: learning to build things

At UJ I spent most of my time learning how systems are built: programming, databases, and how software fits together. Informatics was the part I enjoyed most, because it looked at how information and systems support a business. My final-year project was an e-commerce platform, which meant thinking about the shopper, the shop and the system all at once. I still remember the satisfaction of watching it work end to end for the first time.

Looking back, that project was the closest I came at varsity to business analysis. But I still saw myself as someone who builds things, not someone who works out what should be built. That came later.

## My first job: graduate software developer

Like most graduates, I applied for job after job online and waited. When the offer came for a 12-month graduate programme as a software developer at Infomage Rims Group, I felt huge relief. In January 2019 I walked in for my first day, excited and trying hard not to show how nervous I was.

I built features and fixed bugs in C#, SQL and Python, including updates that closed security gaps. I ran unit and integration tests to find defects before users did. And I kept records of every code change so the rest of the team knew what had moved.

The part that surprised me most was requirements. I helped plan and gather requirements for two learning platforms, Skillzbook and Sigma7, and I saw how much of a developer's time goes into building the wrong thing well. It was frustrating to finish a feature, see it pass every test, and still hear that it wasn't what the user needed.

I didn't have a name for it then, but that frustration was my first taste of business analysis.

## Shell Energy: where data quality really starts

The graduate programme ended after its 12 months, and that was a hard moment. I'd hoped it would lead straight into a permanent developer role. In February 2020 I took a role at Shell Energy as a data capturer. If I'm honest, it felt like a step backwards at first. It wasn't the job I'd pictured when I graduated.

The work was moving paper records onto database systems, so the data was accurate and easy to find. I checked entries for mistakes and fixed them quickly. Before long I noticed the same kinds of errors kept coming back, and fixing them one at a time started to feel pointless.

So I started noting where each mistake came from, and making small changes to how the data was captured. Those changes cut the errors down more than any amount of checking afterwards. That was the first time I felt the quiet pride of fixing a problem at its source.

The lesson stayed with me: if the same problem keeps coming back, look at the process, not just the output.

## Covid, and a pause

Then Covid arrived. When my time at Shell ended in September 2020, I was unemployed, in the middle of a pandemic, with most companies freezing hiring.

Those months were some of the hardest of my life. I'd sit at home sending applications into silence, and there were days I doubted whether my degree had been worth it. Watching friends and family deal with the same uncertainty didn't make it easier.

What kept me going was thinking about the work I'd enjoyed so far. It kept coming back to the same thing: understanding how things work and why they go wrong. I didn't know yet what that job was called, but I knew I wanted to find it.

## Bolt: numbers and people

In January 2021 I joined Bolt South Africa as an operations specialist. Getting that call after months of waiting is a feeling I won't forget.

My job was to go through supply numbers and ride trends and find where performance was slipping. I collected and checked driver and ride data from internal systems before using it. Dirty data had taught me that lesson at Shell. Then I built dashboards and reports that gave the operations team and other stakeholders a clear view of what was happening.

It was a tough period in the market, and driver engagement started dropping. The dashboards showed us where and when. They couldn't tell us why. To understand that, we had to look past the numbers at how drivers were experiencing the platform, and then shape ideas to keep more of them on it and run operations more efficiently.

Bolt was also my first real exposure to BI. I learned how to turn raw data into reports and visuals people could act on, and how to present the insights to the team. The first time I presented my findings, my heart was racing. Seeing the team use what I'd shown them to make a decision gave me a confidence I hadn't felt since varsity.

It's where I learned that data starts the analysis but doesn't finish it. You still need to understand the people behind the numbers, and you need to tell the story clearly.

## What I carried forward

When I moved into business analysis in banking in 2022, it felt like the pieces finally fit. I'd been building towards it the whole time without knowing it.

- From development, I knew what IT needs from a requirement, and how testing proves it.
- From data capture, I knew that quality is designed into a process, not checked in at the end.
- From operations, I knew how to use data to find a problem, and that people explain it.

People, process and technology. I learned the technology first, the process second, and the people third. Business analysis is where all three come together.

If you've just finished your degree and your first job isn't the one you pictured, or you're stuck at home sending applications into silence, I know how heavy that feels. Keep going. Every step I took, even the ones that felt like steps backwards, taught me something I use today.
