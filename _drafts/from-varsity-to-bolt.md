When I finished my BSc in Computer Science and Informatics at the University of Johannesburg in 2018, I thought I knew exactly where I was going. I'd write software. That's what the degree was for, and that's what I'd spent three years preparing to do.

It didn't quite work out that way, and I'm glad it didn't.

## Varsity: learning to build things

At UJ I spent most of my time learning how systems are built: programming, databases, and how software fits together. Informatics was the part I enjoyed most, because it looked at how information and systems support a business. My final-year project was an e-commerce platform, which meant thinking about the shopper, the shop and the system all at once.

Looking back, that project was the closest I came at varsity to business analysis. But I still thought of myself as someone who builds things, not someone who works out what should be built. That came later.

## My first job: graduate software developer

I applied for jobs online like most graduates, and in January 2019 I started on a 12-month graduate programme as a software developer at Infomage Rims Group.

I built features and fixed bugs in C#, SQL and Python, including updates that closed security gaps. I ran unit and integration tests to find defects before users did. And I kept records of every code change so the rest of the team knew what had moved.

The part that surprised me most was requirements. I helped plan and gather requirements for two learning platforms, Skillzbook and Sigma7, and I saw how much of a developer's time goes into building the wrong thing well. A feature could pass every test and still miss what the user needed, because the need was never pinned down properly.

I didn't have a name for it then, but that was my first taste of business analysis.

## Shell Energy: where data quality really starts

The graduate programme ended after its 12 months, and in February 2020 I took a role at Shell Energy as a data capturer. It wasn't the developer role I'd pictured, but it was work, and it turned out to teach me a lot.

The work was moving paper records onto database systems, so the data was accurate and easy to find. I checked entries for mistakes and fixed them quickly. Before long I noticed the same kinds of errors kept coming back.

So I started noting where each mistake came from, and making small changes to how the data was captured. Those changes cut the errors down more than any amount of checking afterwards.

That taught me something I still use: if the same problem keeps coming back, look at the process, not just the output.

## Covid, and a pause

Then Covid arrived. When my time at Shell ended in September 2020, I was unemployed, in the middle of a pandemic, with most companies freezing hiring. Those months were hard. They also gave me time to think about what I actually enjoyed in the work so far, and it kept coming back to the same thing: understanding how things work and why they go wrong.

## Bolt: numbers and people

In January 2021 I joined Bolt South Africa as an operations specialist.

My job was to go through supply numbers and ride trends and find where performance was slipping. I collected and checked driver and ride data from internal systems before using it. Dirty data had taught me that lesson at Shell. Then I built dashboards and reports that gave the operations team and other stakeholders a clear view of what was happening.

It was a tough period in the market, and driver engagement started dropping. The dashboards showed us where and when. They couldn't tell us why. To understand that, we had to look past the numbers at how drivers were experiencing the platform, and then shape ideas to keep more of them on it and run operations more efficiently.

Bolt was also my first real exposure to BI. I learned how to turn raw data into reports and visuals people could act on, and how to present the insights to the team so they led to a decision rather than just a nod. It's where I learned that data starts the analysis but doesn't finish it. You still need to understand the people behind the numbers, and you need to tell the story clearly.

## What I carried forward

When I moved into business analysis in banking in 2022, I realised I'd been building towards it the whole time.

- From development, I knew what IT needs from a requirement, and how testing proves it.
- From data capture, I knew that quality is designed into a process, not checked in at the end.
- From operations, I knew how to use data to find a problem, and that people explain it.

People, process and technology. I learned the technology first, the process second, and the people third. Business analysis is where all three come together.

If you've just finished your degree and your first job isn't the one you pictured, don't worry too much. Mine wasn't either, and every step taught me something I use today.
